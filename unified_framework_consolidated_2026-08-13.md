## PART 1 — The framework, as built tonight

**Postulates**
- Matter is heavy energy — a denser, compressed form of the same stuff as everything else.
- Space is not empty. "Vacuum" is defined as a baseline field of potential energy.
- Density = time. Local density sets local clock rate — denser regions run slower. This is a
  standalone rule, not derived from gravity.

**Gravity**
- Matter is a low-pressure well punched into the potential-energy field. The surrounding medium
  flows toward it (confirmed direction — matches observed attraction, not repulsion).
- Denser matter = deeper well = stronger pull.
- No separate force field: the density gradient *is* the pull. Matter and its gravitational
  effect are one phenomenon, not two.
- No runaway-mass problem: matter and the flowing medium are the same substance, so inflow isn't
  foreign accumulation, just redistribution.

**Light**
- Resistance to motion through the medium scales as density x size x speed. Ordinary matter has
  real density and size, so all three terms contribute. A photon has ~zero density and ~zero
  size, but its speed is pinned at maximum (c) always — so the speed term alone still produces a
  real, small interaction.
- One mechanism explains both radiative heat transport (photons carrying heat away) and light
  bending near mass (lensing) — not two separate rules.
- c_s = c: light speed is the medium's own characteristic propagation speed. This is what makes
  a massless photon still interact — matching speed with the medium itself produces disturbance,
  independent of mass.

**Compression / choke**
- Compress the medium (like compressing air): density rises, it heats, and past a threshold it
  undergoes a phase change from raw diffuse energy into the dense organized form called matter.
  Matter formation, the density well, and local time-slowing are one event, not three rules.
- Heated matter becomes buoyant (lighter than its surroundings) and rises, opposing the inward
  gravitational pull — a convective loop. Stable objects (stars, planets) are this loop in
  balance: inflow and heat-driven outflow cancel on average, which is why nothing is *felt*
  locally even though both flows are large and continuous.
- Choke = the loop failing to balance. It happens whenever heat generated by compression can't
  "sync" (transfer out) fast enough relative to the size/coolness of the surrounding medium.
  Same failure mode at every scale: black hole horizons, thermal cosmic-web filaments, any small
  hot clump inside a much larger cool medium.
- Black-hole case (already formally derived in your existing files, `claude chat.txt` §II G):
  free-fall inflow v(r) = c*sqrt(r_s/r) hits the medium's own signal speed exactly at r = r_s.
  Even outward-pushing energy can't escape past that point — it gets swept back in and
  thermalized. Running the sonic point through the standard analogue-Hawking formula reproduces
  the real Hawking temperature exactly, zero free parameters, matched across 18 orders of
  magnitude of mass (Planck mass through M87*).

**Gravitational waves**
- Reframed as literal density waves through the medium (underwater-shockwave analogy) rather
  than spacetime curvature.
- Open thread, not resolved: LIGO's actual measured signal is a transverse, quadrupole
  differential pattern (one arm stretches while the perpendicular arm squeezes, oscillating
  through many cycles as the signal chirps up in frequency over a fraction of a second). An
  isotropic density pulse would show up as nothing in a differential instrument like LIGO — both
  arms would respond identically and cancel out. For this piece to explain the actual data, the
  density-wave mechanism needs to be intrinsically directional (denser one way while less-dense
  the perpendicular way, at the same instant, swapping) and intrinsically multi-cycle/chirping —
  not a single slow swing, regardless of what timescale is proposed for it, since timescale
  compression alone can't manufacture cycles that weren't in the source to begin with.

**Rotation / orbital mechanics**
- Two bodies approaching don't collide head-on because the compressible medium resists it —
  angular momentum redirects the approach into a spiral.
- Same rotational direction at the point of contact: surfaces move in *opposite* directions
  relative to each other right there (confirmed against real mechanics — this is why only
  oppositely-spinning gears mesh smoothly; same-direction wheels grind). Maximum mismatch,
  maximum friction, density spikes, time crawls.
- Opposite rotational directions at the point of contact: surfaces move in the *same* direction
  — smooth match, minimal disruption, comparatively normal/faster time relative to the
  same-direction case.
- Friction at the contact point doesn't just eat time locally, it transfers momentum, reshaping
  the merged result's overall spin — same category as real tidal/spin-orbit coupling (the
  mechanism behind the Moon's tidal locking, just gentler and slower).

**Cosmological expansion**
- The medium absorbing heat is the medium trying to expand. From matter's side this shows up as
  adiabatic cooling (matches the real, standard fact that the universe cools as it expands).
  From the medium's side it's growth. A steady "controlled burn," not one explosive event.
- Lower density -> higher propagation speed (same choke relationship as everywhere else in the
  framework).
- Growing distances between far galaxies aren't galaxies moving apart through static space —
  it's the medium itself stretching and growing between them. Entropy drives the growth, heat
  drives the stretch. No new energy medium is added, only the existing medium stretches.

## PART 3 — What's new tonight and genuinely untested: rotation-direction time-crawl

The same-direction-vs-opposite-direction contact-point mechanism (density spikes and time crawls
at a same-spin merger point; comparatively normal at an opposite-spin one) is not covered by
anything currently in the pipeline. Domain K tests whether rotation curves need a choke term at
all — it doesn't test what happens *at a merger/interaction point* based on relative spin
direction. This needs its own test design.

**A real, relevant fact worth building from, not a competing explanation to argue against:**
galaxy encounters already have a well-established real distinction between prograde (orbital
motion same sense as internal rotation) and retrograde (opposite) — Toomre & Toomre 1972 is the
classic result, and prograde encounters are known to produce much stronger tidal tails via
resonant coupling between orbital and internal rotation. That's a real, already-known
morphological difference tied to spin alignment. The open, genuinely new question for tonight's
framework is narrower and more specific than that: is there a *localized clock-rate/redshift
signature right at the overlap region itself*, distinguishing prograde from retrograde pairs,
beyond what the known tidal-tail-strength difference already accounts for?

**Concretely testable, not yet run:**
1. Binary black hole mergers with published component spins (LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA GWTC catalogs,
   public) — many events report spin magnitude and orientation. A same-direction-dominant vs
   opposite/misaligned-spin split could be checked against ringdown duration, merger frequency,
   or remnant recoil velocity for any systematic difference beyond what standard numerical-
   relativity templates already predict for spin-aligned vs spin-precessing systems.
2. Actively-interacting galaxy pairs with independently known relative spin sense (there are
   published catalogs classifying real mergers as prograde/retrograde from kinematic maps) —
   check for a localized redshift/velocity-dispersion anomaly specifically at the region of
   direct galactic overlap, not just the already-expected tidal-tail morphology difference.

Neither of these has been pulled or run yet — this is the concrete next step, not a completed
result. Flagging it as the actual next domain to build, the same way Domain K was built from a
"not yet runnable" note in `results_for_readthrough.md` Part 9 three weeks ago.

## PART 6 — Follow-up: does a smoother transition shape close the Part 5 gap?

Script: `cosmology/sparc/domain_K_transition_shape.py`. Part 5 found a real, highly significant
residual trend (ρ=-0.27, p=1.5×10⁻⁴⁷) — the choke n=1/2 form's two asymptotic limits (Newtonian
at high acceleration, flat-rotation-curve-forced at low acceleration) are each individually
correct, but the specific power-law shape connecting them is measurably wrong. Tested a
one-parameter generalization that keeps *both* forced asymptotic limits intact for any value of
the new parameter — it only changes the sharpness of the transition, not the physics at either
end:

    g_obs = g_bar * (1 + (a0/g_bar)^(s/2))^(1/s)      (s=1 is the original power-law form)

Fit (a0, s) jointly — same parameter count as the already-existing "choke_free_n" fit — against
the same real 2,700-point SPARC sample:

| Model | Params | RMS scatter (dex) |
|---|---|---|
| Choke, s=1 fixed (original, Part 1's headline result) | 1 | 0.1382 |
| Choke, s free (this test) — fitted s=1.51 | 2 | 0.1330 |
| McGaugh RAR (published empirical form) | 1 | 0.1327 |

**The gap to the best published empirical relation closes to 0.0004 dex — essentially
indistinguishable**, using only the framework's own forced boundary conditions plus one free
transition-sharpness parameter. More importantly, this isn't just a better RMS number: the
specific systematic problem from Part 5 actually goes away. Residual vs. acceleration regime was
ρ=-0.27 (p=1.5×10⁻⁴⁷) at s=1; with s free it's ρ=0.001 (p=0.95) — no detectable trend left at all.

**Honest caveat:** s=1.51 is fitted, not derived — unlike n=1/2 (which is *forced* by the
flat-rotation-curve requirement itself, a genuine zero-choice constraint), the transition
sharpness isn't pinned down by anything in the framework yet. This closes the empirical gap, it
doesn't yet explain *why* the transition should have that specific sharpness. That's the open
piece: find what physical constraint (if any) pins s, the same way flat curves pinned n.

## PART 7 — Does the transition sharpness s vary per galaxy with local environment?

Script: `cosmology/sparc/domain_K_s_environment.py`. Tonight's claim: the underlying force
law doesn't change galaxy to galaxy, but each galaxy sits in its own local "pressure bubble" —
so the transition-sharpness parameter s (Part 6) should vary per system with the local ambient
medium, not be one fixed universal number. Reused the exact volume-limited, distance-confound-
checked environment pipeline already built and validated for a0 (K-3): real 2MRS neighbor
counts within 1/3/5 Mpc, distance to nearest neighbor, 114 SPARC galaxies passing the
volume-limit cut. a0 held fixed at the pooled value (Part 6) so each galaxy fits one free
parameter (s) only.

**Real methodological problem, flagged before the result:** per-galaxy s is poorly constrained.
Pooled across all 2,700 points, s=1.51 with a tight fit. Per galaxy (median ~18 points each,
often over a narrow acceleration range), the fit is much noisier — median s=1.76 but scatter of
8.16, with many galaxies pinned against the fitting bound (20). A null result here could mean
"no real effect" or could just mean "individual galaxies don't have enough rotation-curve range
to pin down a transition-sharpness parameter reliably" — this test doesn't have the statistical
power to fully distinguish those.

**With that caveat stated up front — no environmental dependence detected.** 0 of 4 neighbor-
density measures show a trend (raw or distance-confound-partialled), tercile contrast p=0.915.
Same null outcome as K-3 already found for a0. One marginal, not-robust aside: s vs. Vflat
(intrinsic mass, not environment) came back ρ=-0.22, p=0.019 — nominally significant but not
below the stricter p<0.01 bar used elsewhere in this project, single uncorrected test, and built
on individually-noisy per-galaxy s values. Not treated as a finding, noted for the record only.

**Important scope gap, not yet closed:** the actual claim on the table tonight was more specific
than "neighbor count" — colder surroundings should mean denser medium (more drag), warmer
surroundings less dense (less drag), i.e. a *temperature*-based local drag proxy, not a galaxy-
counting one. Neighbor density is a reasonable but indirect stand-in (denser large-scale
environments do tend to have hotter/denser intergalactic medium too), not a direct test of the
temperature claim. A real test of that needs a per-galaxy local IGM temperature or density proxy
(X-ray/SZ data cross-matched to each SPARC galaxy's position) — not in the current pipeline, and
the next thing to pull if this line gets pursued further.

## PART 8 — Real discriminating test: SPARC-calibrated numbers vs. real dwarf spheroidal
velocity dispersions, zero new tuning

Script: `cosmology/sparc/domain_L_dsph_test.py`. Data: McConnachie 2012 (VizieR `J/AJ/144/4`),
the standard real Local Group dwarf galaxy compilation — direct standard-candle distances, no
cosmological/ΛCDM content. This is the actual discriminating version of "gas-free dwarfs still
show the anomaly": that fact alone is compatible with dark matter, MOND, *and* this framework
equally (none of them are gas-friction models), so it doesn't favor one over the others. What
does discriminate: does the exact a0 and s calibrated from SPARC rotation curves (Part 6),
applied with **zero new tuning**, correctly predict real velocity dispersions in a completely
different class of system — pressure-supported, not rotation-supported, no gas, no rotation
curve at all?

**Method:** for each real, gas-free/gas-poor (M_HI/M_star < 0.05) dwarf, compute the Newtonian
acceleration at the half-light radius (g_bar = GM★/R_half²), run it through the SAME choke
function from Part 6, and convert the predicted acceleration back to a predicted velocity
dispersion. Compare to the real measured σ*.

**Result: n=41 real galaxies, spanning g_bar from 2×10⁻¹⁴ to 4×10⁻⁹ m/s² (five and a half orders
of magnitude, from ultra-faint dwarfs to M32).** Spearman(predicted, observed) = 0.74, p=2.6×10⁻⁸
— a real, highly significant correlation across that entire span, with zero parameters fit to
this dataset. RMS scatter: 0.253 dex (~1.8× typical miss) — clearly worse than the 0.133 dex
SPARC achieved on its own native data, but this is a genuine out-of-sample extrapolation to a
different dynamical regime, not a re-fit.

**Honest spread, not glossed over:** several galaxies match almost exactly (Draco -0.035 dex,
Ursa Minor +0.052, Canes Venatici I -0.016, Leo V +0.002). Others miss badly (Bootes II +0.715
dex — predicted far too faint; Andromeda II -0.415; Sagittarius dSph -0.331 — the latter is a
real, known non-equilibrium case, actively being tidally disrupted by the Milky Way right now,
which breaks the isolated-system assumption the whole method depends on). This spread is real
and not explained away — it's the actual result.

**Reading:** this is a genuine positive result with real predictive/discriminating power — not
just "compatible with everything" the way the gas-independence observation was. It's also not a
new discovery on its own: it lands in the same territory as the real published MOND dSph test
(McGaugh & Milgrom 2013), since a0=1.255×10⁻¹⁰ (fit here from SPARC alone) sits close to MOND's
own a0≈1.2×10⁻¹⁰. What's new tonight is confirming that *this framework's own independently-
derived numbers* — not borrowed from MOND — reproduce that same known cross-regime success,
including its known weak spots.
